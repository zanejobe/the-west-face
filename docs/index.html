Act II · Research time

Early exploration

Reconnaissance and first systematic observations.

1854
Mapping

Bartlett — Personal Narrative of Explorations (Guadalupes)

John Russell Bartlett, U.S.–Mexico Boundary Commissioner (1850–53), publishes his two-volume Personal Narrative. Approaching the western escarpment in November 1850 he describes Guadalupe Mountain as a dark, gloomy-looking range with bold and forbidding sides — among the earliest published eyewitness accounts of the west face; Bartlett Peak is later named for him.

John Russell Bartlett

Bartlett, J.R., 1854, Personal Narrative of Explorations and Incidents in Texas, New Mexico, California, Sonora, and Chihuahua, Connected with the United States and Mexican Boundary Commission, during the years 1850, '51, '52, and '53: New York, D. Appleton & Company, 2 vols., https://archive.org/details/personalnarrativ01bart

1858
PaleontologyMapping

Shumard — Permian fossils from the Guadalupes

G. G. Shumard visited the Texas Guadalupes with Capt. John Pope's survey; fossils he collected were identified by B. F. Shumard as resembling Permian faunas of Europe — among the earliest geologic observations of the range.

G. G. Shumard, B. F. Shumard

Shumard, B.F., 1858, Notice of fossils from the Permian strata of Texas and New Mexico obtained by the United States expedition under Capt. John Pope…: Transactions of the Academy of Science of St. Louis, v. 1 (volume date often given as 1860). Context summarized in Hayes (1964, USGS PP 446, Introduction).

1904
MappingStratigraphyDelaware Mountain GroupCapitan Limestone

Richardson names Delaware Mountain and Capitan formations

G. B. Richardson's Trans-Pecos reconnaissance names the Delaware Mountain Formation (sandstone and limestone of the Delaware and Guadalupe Mountains) and Capitan Limestone — the parent nomenclature later split into the Delaware Mountain Group.

G. B. Richardson

Richardson, G.B., 1904, Report of a reconnaissance in Trans-Pecos Texas north of the Texas and Pacific Railway: University of Texas Mineral Survey Bulletin 9, 119 p. (Geolex original reference for Delaware Mountain).

1908
PaleontologyStratigraphyDelaware Mountain GroupCapitan Limestone

Girty — USGS Professional Paper 58 The Guadalupian Fauna

George H. Girty's USGS Professional Paper 58 monographs fossils from Capitan and Delaware Mountain strata of the southern Guadalupe Mountains — the classic paleontologic baseline for Guadalupian faunas.

George H. Girty

Girty, G.H., 1908, The Guadalupian Fauna: USGS Professional Paper 58, 651 p., https://doi.org/10.3133/pp58

1924
MappingStratigraphyDelaware Mountain Group

Beede geologic map of part of West Texas

BEG syntheses credit Beede (1924) with recognizing a lithologic tripartite character in Delaware Mountain sandstone that later underpinned King's three-formation subdivision. Primary item is University of Texas Bulletin 2607.

J. W. Beede

Beede, J.W., 1924, Geologic map of a part of West Texas: University of Texas Bulletin 2607. Tripartite role as summarized by BEG DMG synthesis (Dutton et al.).

King — Brushy Cherry and Bell Canyon named

P. B. King describes the Delaware Mountain Group and its Brushy Canyon, Cherry Canyon, and Bell Canyon Formations (names already credited to him in DeFord and Lloyd, 1940). Formal lithostratigraphic frame for the western escarpment and Delaware Basin fill.

Act II · Research time

Mid-century frameworks

Stratigraphic refinement, sedimentology, and basin models.

1974
SedimentologyBasin fillBrushy Canyon Formation

Harms — Brushy Canyon deep-water density-current deposit

J. C. Harms interprets the Brushy Canyon Formation as a deep-water density-current deposit (GSA Bulletin). Widely cited turning point in Brushy Canyon process models.

John C. Harms

Harms, J.C., 1974, Brushy Canyon Formation, Texas: A deep-water density current deposit: Geological Society of America Bulletin, v. 85, p. 1763–1784.

1988
SedimentologyBasin fillPetroleumBrushy Canyon FormationBell Canyon FormationDelaware Mountain Group

Harms and Williamson — DMG saline density currents

Harms and Williamson argue Delaware Mountain Group siltstones and channelized sandstones (including Brushy and Bell Canyon) were deposited by saline density currents in a density-stratified basin, contrasting with turbidity-current submarine-fan models.

John C. Harms, Charles R. Williamson

Harms, J.C., and Williamson, C.R., 1988, Deep-water density current deposits of Delaware Mountain Group (Permian), Delaware Basin, Texas and New Mexico: AAPG Bulletin, v. 72, p. 299–317.
